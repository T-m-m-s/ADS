Concentration of S&P 500 index

In this data challege we study how concentrated the S&P 500 is among its constituent companies.

  1. The S&P 500 (Standard & Poor’s 500) is a stock market index that tracks the performance of 500 of the largest publicly traded companies in the United States.
  2. It covers roughly 80% of the total value of the U.S. stock market.
  3. It is a market-capitalization-weighted index. Larger companies have a much bigger impact on its value than smaller components

We will use:

library(tidyverse)
library(tidyquant)
library(ineq)

The three packages have different roles:

tidyquant    retrieve the S&P 500 constituents and weights
tidyverse    manipulate and visualize the data
ineq         calculate the Lorenz curve and Gini coefficient

Our main questions are:

  1. How unequal are the company weights in the S&P 500?
  2. How much of the index is represented by its largest companies?
  3. What does the Lorenz curve tell us about concentration?
  4. What is the Gini coefficient of the S&P 500 weights?

A small number of companies carries a large share of the index: the top 10 companies alone account for about 37% of the total weight, the top 20 for about 49% and the top 30 for about 55%, out of roughly 500 constituents. The exact figures change slightly over time because the constituents and weights are retrieved live.

3.

lorenz_curve <- Lc(df$weight)

plot(lorenz_curve, col = "steelblue", lwd = 2, main = "Lorenz curve - S&P 500 weights", xlab = "Company percent", ylab = "Cumulative percent of weight")

The diagonal line shows perfect equality, where every company has the same weight. The function Lc sorts the companies by weight in increasing order, so the curve shows the cumulative share of weight held by the smallest companies. The curve lies far below the diagonal and only rises steeply at the very end: the smaller half of the companies holds only about 9% of the total weight, while the largest 10% holds about 63%. The weights are therefore highly concentrated in a few large companies.

4.

gini_value <- ineq(df$weight, type = "Gini")

print(gini_value)
## [1] 0.7092338

The Gini coefficient measures, on a scale from 0 to 1, how unequally a variable is distributed. A value of 0 means perfect equality (all companies have the same weight), while 1 means maximum inequality (a single company holds all the weight and the others have none). Here the Gini coefficient of the weights is about 0.71, which indicates strong concentration: a few companies have a large weight and many have a very small one, as the Lorenz curve also shows.
